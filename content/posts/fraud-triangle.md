Welcome to the first installment of a series nobody asked for and HR
would very much like me to stop writing. We're talking about fraud --
the theory of it, the mechanics of it, the glorious, avoidable
stupidity of it -- across banking, corporate accounting, and organized
retail theft. I promise you an unreasonable amount of detail. Where
that detail comes from is between me and my lawyer, and no, that's not
a bit, that's just a boundary.

Let's start at the beginning, with the closest thing this field has to
a periodic table: the **Fraud Triangle**. Every scheme you will ever
read about, no matter how baroque, boils down to three ingredients
sitting in the same room at the same time. Take any one of them away
and the whole thing quietly fails to happen. This is, coincidentally,
also why "just hire honest people" has never once worked as a fraud
prevention strategy -- honesty is not a fixed trait, it's a variable
that moves depending on how the other two legs of the triangle are
doing that week.

The concept comes from Donald Cressey, a criminologist who in the
1950s did the very sensible thing of just asking convicted embezzlers
why they did it, instead of guessing. Turns out people who steal from
their employers are, shockingly, willing to explain their reasoning in
great detail once there's no more upside to keeping quiet. Somebody
should try that approach more often.

## Pressure: the "non-shareable problem"

Every fraud starts with a problem the person doesn't feel they can
say out loud. Gambling losses. A second mortgage nobody at the office
knows about. A lifestyle that quietly outgrew the salary funding it. A
bonus target invented by someone in a much nicer office who will never
have to explain how it gets hit. The word doing all the work here is
*non-shareable* -- not "the person has a problem," but "the person has
a problem they've decided they'd rather commit a felony over than
mention to their manager, their spouse, or a financial advisor."

This is why "our people would tell us if something was wrong" is one
of the great punchlines of corporate risk management. They wouldn't.
That's the entire premise. The problem is non-shareable specifically
*to the people who could actually help.* A functioning EAP program
catches more fraud, dollar for dollar, than most compliance training
ever will, and almost nobody budgets for it that way.

Pressure is also the one leg of the triangle you, the organization,
mostly can't see coming and mostly can't control. You don't get to
audit somebody's marriage or their sports book account. Which is
exactly why the smart money -- and by "smart money" I mean people who
have thought about this far more carefully than a rational person
should -- focuses defensive effort on the other two legs instead.

## Opportunity: the only leg you actually control

This is the fun one. Opportunity is the belief -- correct or not -- that
the scheme can be executed and concealed. And unlike pressure and
rationalization, which live entirely inside somebody's skull where you
have no jurisdiction, opportunity lives in your process design, which
you absolutely do control and routinely leave lying around like an
unlocked bike.

Opportunity shows up as:

- **One person with both custody and recordkeeping.** The same
  person who receives the cash also reconciles the cash. The same
  person who approves the vendor also cuts the vendor's check. Anyone
  who has ever taken an intro auditing class will tell you this is the
  cardinal sin of internal controls, and yet it is *everywhere*,
  because segregation of duties costs headcount and headcount costs
  budget and budget is somebody else's problem this quarter.
- **Reconciliation theater.** A control that exists on an org chart
  and a compliance binder but that nobody has actually performed with
  their eyes open in eleven months. Deeply comforting to auditors who
  only check that the checkbox exists, not that anyone meant it.
- **Trust standing in for verification.** "Dave's been here
  nineteen years, he'd never." Dave has been here nineteen years
  specifically because nobody has ever checked Dave's work, and Dave
  noticed that around year three.
- **Override authority with no counterweight.** Somebody senior
  enough to approve their own exceptions. This one shows up constantly
  once we get to the accounting fraud posts -- it's basically the
  entire Enron plot in one bullet point.

None of this requires the fraudster to be a criminal mastermind. Most
aren't. Most fraud is not *Ocean's Eleven*, it's a mediocre plan that
worked purely because nobody was checking. This is either deeply
reassuring or deeply terrifying depending on whether you're reading
this as a business owner or, hypothetically, someone with a more
colorful employment history.

Case in point, and a genuine gift to anyone teaching this concept:
Thomas Frudaker, arrested in Yuma, Arizona in 2018 after a store
employee noticed something off about a computer he'd just returned.
"Off" turned out to mean gutted -- the valuable internal components
quietly removed, the shell reassembled to look untouched, then handed
back across the counter for a full refund. Investigators believe he'd
been running the identical play at Walmart stores nationwide for about
eighteen months, an estimated **thousand-plus locations**, netting
something like **$1.3 million** before one employee at one register
finally opened the box. Not a hacking operation, not a criminal
syndicate -- one guy, a screwdriver, and a return desk that verified
"is there a computer-shaped object in this box" rather than "is this
the computer that was sold." That gap between the two is opportunity,
in its purest and most poorly-defended form. Eighteen months is a long
time to profit off a policy nobody thought to close.

I bring this up not to admire the technique -- perish the thought -- but
because it's the cleanest illustration you'll find of why "opportunity"
gets top billing among the three legs. Pressure and rationalization
are guesswork. Opportunity is a policy decision, and this particular
policy decision cost a trillion-dollar retailer over a million dollars
because verifying a return took longer than trusting it.

## Rationalization: the bedtime story you tell yourself

The last ingredient is the one people underrate, because it sounds
like a soft, feelings-based footnote next to "stole $400,000." It
isn't. Rationalization is the mental permission slip that lets someone
commit fraud and still show up the next morning believing they're
fundamentally a good person. Nobody -- and I do mean nobody -- sits down
and thinks "I am now going to be a thief." They think:

- "I'm borrowing it. I'll put it back before anyone checks."
- "They underpay me for what I actually do here. I'm owed this."
- "Everyone in this industry does some version of this, I'm just
  the one being honest about it, in the sense of doing it and not
  discussing it."
- "The company makes plenty. This isn't going to hurt anyone."
- "I'm smarter than the people whose job it is to catch this,
  and being right about that isn't a crime."

That last one is doing a lot of quiet, structural work, by the way.
Confidence in one's own cleverness is practically load-bearing in this
field -- ask literally anyone who's spent time around people who do
this for a living. Not that I have. Allegedly.

Rationalization matters because it's the difference between fraud and
ordinary theft, psychologically speaking. A mugger doesn't need a
philosophy. A fraudster generally does, because they have to look
themselves in the mirror, go to their kid's soccer game, and sit
through Thanksgiving dinner. The story doesn't have to be *good.* It
just has to be good enough to get them through the next reflection in
the bathroom mirror.

## Why this triangle is the whole syllabus

Here's the practical payoff, and the reason this isn't just true-crime
trivia dressed up as theory: whenever you look at any fraud scheme --
a bank teller running fake accounts, a CFO booking revenue that
doesn't exist yet, a booster ring fencing stolen retail goods through
a marketplace storefront -- and you ask "what was the pressure, what
was the opportunity, what was the rationalization," the entire
mechanism falls out cleanly, every single time. It's a diagnostic, not
a decoration.

It also tells you exactly where your money is best spent defensively,
which is the part actual fraud examiners get paid to know and actual
executives routinely ignore in favor of a training video nobody
watches past minute two. You cannot screen job applicants for future
gambling debt. You cannot audit someone's internal monologue for
rationalization quality. But you can absolutely, unglamorously, close
down opportunity -- segregate duties, verify instead of trust,
reconcile for real, remove unilateral override authority -- and that
one leg of the triangle does more preventive work than the other two
combined. This is not a hot take. This is close to the entire field of
internal controls in one sentence, and consultants will bill you
five figures to tell you the same thing with a nicer slide deck.

One more wrinkle before we move on, because the original three-legged
model has taken some deserved criticism over the decades: later
researchers proposed a fourth leg, capability -- the idea that pulling
off a *sophisticated* fraud (as opposed to a clumsy one that gets
caught in a week) takes more than motive and opportunity. It takes the
actual skill to design the scheme, the composure to execute it under
pressure, and the social confidence to talk your way past questions
when someone finally gets suspicious. This became the "Fraud Diamond,"
and it's worth filing away now, because it's going to matter a great
deal once we get into the accounting-fraud posts, where the people
involved were rarely improvising.
